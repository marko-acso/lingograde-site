## 1. What Camp Is (And What It Isn't)

Jim Camp's negotiation system begins with a single premise: **the person who needs the outcome least holds the power.** In LingoGrade's context, this means the assessor who needs the student to buy, rebook, or tip is already compromised. The session quality drops. The student feels it. Trust evaporates.

Camp is not indifference. It is **zero emotional investment in the outcome combined with maximum investment in the process.** You care deeply about delivering an excellent session. You do not care at all whether the student buys a homework check afterwards. These two positions are not contradictory — they are the foundation of trust.

### The Grandmother Analogy

Imagine a grandmother watching a child play in the garden. She is warm, present, attentive, and genuinely delighted. She is also completely unattached to what the child chooses to do next. If the child picks up a ball, wonderful. If the child sits in the mud, also wonderful. The grandmother does not need the child to perform.

That is your emotional posture during every session.

### Camp vs Voss — How They Work Together

| Voss (Tactical Empathy) | Camp (Start with No) |
|--------------------------|----------------------|
| Labels emotions to defuse them | Removes your need for any particular emotional response |
| Builds trust through demonstrated understanding | Builds trust through demonstrated non-attachment |
| Gives you the words | Gives you the posture |
| Operates at the sentence level | Operates at the identity level |

You need both. Voss without Camp produces a skilled manipulator. Camp without Voss produces a competent robot. Together, they produce the Tactical Empath described in Section 1 of the Assessor Rules.

## 2. The Five Pillars of Camp Protocol

### Pillar 1: Vision Drives Decision

People do not buy products. They buy a vision of their future self. Your job is not to describe LingoGrade's features. Your job is to help the student see what their language ability could look like — and then get out of the way.

**In practice:**
- "Students at your level who work on this specific pattern tend to see it click within a few weeks."
- "Imagine walking into that meeting in Munich and not needing the interpreter."

You are painting a picture. You are not selling a ticket to it.

### Pillar 2: No Need for the Deal

This is the hardest pillar for most assessors because it conflicts with every instinct about "conversion." But the data is unambiguous: students who feel no pressure convert at significantly higher rates than students who feel even subtle pressure.

**The test:** After your session, could you honestly say "If this student never books again, I delivered something valuable today"? If yes, you are in Camp. If no, you are selling.

### Pillar 3: Let Them Say No

Camp teaches that "no" is not the end of a conversation — it is the beginning of safety. A student who feels free to say no will eventually say yes from a position of genuine desire rather than social obligation.

**In practice:**
- "Would it be wrong to see how these patterns change in eight weeks?" (No-oriented question — the answer "no, it wouldn't be wrong" is actually a yes.)
- "If the homework check doesn't feel right for you, skip it entirely." (Explicit permission to decline.)

### Pillar 4: Budget Awareness

Camp identifies four budgets every person manages: time, energy, money, and emotion. Respecting all four means:

- **Time:** Never run over. Never imply they need to spend more time than they want.
- **Energy:** Match their energy level. A drained corporate student at 6 PM needs a different pace than a motivated teenager at 10 AM.
- **Money:** State prices once, factually, without justification. "The homework check is 29.95" — not "It's only 29.95" or "For just 29.95."
- **Emotion:** Never guilt. Never create anxiety about their level. Never imply they are falling behind.

### Pillar 5: Blank Slate

Enter every session without assumptions. The student who booked a Quick Assessment might be C1. The corporate group might include a native speaker who was sent by HR. The "beginner" might have spent two years watching German television and can understand everything but produce nothing.

"I have no idea where you are. Let's find out together."

This is not a technique. This is the literal truth. You do not know until you listen.

## The Psychological Framework

Camp gives you the posture. Voss gives you the words. But four additional frameworks explain **what is happening inside the student's mind** during your session — and why Camp works at a neurological level. Understanding these will make your Camp instincts sharper.

### Berne: Transactional Analysis — The Ego State Map

Eric Berne identified three ego states that every person shifts between: **Parent** (rules, judgments, "you should"), **Adult** (facts, curiosity, "let's look at this"), and **Child** (play, creativity, emotion, vulnerability).

Your job is to operate from **Adult** and invite the student's **Free Child** — the part of them that speaks without self-monitoring, takes risks with vocabulary, and forgets they are being assessed.

**What threatens this:**
- **Critical Parent** from the assessor: correcting grammar mid-sentence, raising an eyebrow at a mistake, saying "actually, it's..." — all of these trigger the student's **Adapted Child** (compliance, shame, silence). You get performance, not acquisition. The data is useless.
- **Nurturing Parent** overload: excessive praise ("Wow, great job!") also breaks the dynamic. The student starts performing *for your approval* rather than speaking naturally. They shift from Free Child to Adapted Child — smiling, agreeable, but no longer authentic.

**The target dynamic:**

| You (Assessor) | Student |
|----------------|---------|
| Adult | Free Child |
| Curious, calm, observational | Playful, unselfconscious, risk-taking |
| "Tell me more about that." | "Well, I think maybe... yeah, so basically..." |
| No evaluation signals | No performance anxiety |

**In practice:**
- Replace "Good!" with genuine engagement: "That's interesting — what happened next?"
- Replace "Can you try that again?" with a natural redirect: "So what did they say when you told them?"
- If the student self-corrects and looks at you for validation, give none. Nod warmly and continue the conversation. You are not a judge. You are weather.

### Krashen: What You Are Actually Measuring

Stephen Krashen's distinction between **acquisition** (unconscious, natural absorption) and **learning** (conscious rule memorisation) is the foundation of what LingoGrade assesses.

**You are measuring acquisition.** You are measuring what the student can produce when they are *not thinking about grammar*. This is why the session must feel like a conversation, not a test — because the moment the student activates their **Monitor** (Krashen's term for the conscious grammar-checker), you are no longer measuring their natural ability. You are measuring their ability to self-edit under pressure.

**The Affective Filter:**
Krashen's Affective Filter Hypothesis states that anxiety, low motivation, and low self-confidence create a mental barrier that blocks language acquisition. During your session, this filter must stay as low as possible. Every Camp principle — no pressure, no judgment, BYAF, blank slate — exists to keep the filter down.

**Signs the filter is up (you caused this):**
- Student starts sentences, stops, restarts with a different structure
- Student asks "Is that correct?" or "How do you say...?" mid-flow
- Long pauses before every sentence (monitoring, not thinking)
- Student switches to their native language apologetically ("Sorry, I don't know how to say this")

**Signs the filter is down (you are doing your job):**
- Student speaks in longer stretches without looking at you for approval
- Student makes errors they don't notice (this is *good* — it means they are in acquisition mode)
- Student laughs, gestures, leans forward
- Student loses track of time

When the filter is down, you are getting real data. When the filter is up, you are getting a performance. Camp keeps the filter down. That is why Camp is not a nice-to-have — it is the measurement methodology.

### Csikszentmihalyi: Flow and the Challenge-Skill Balance

Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi's Flow research shows that people enter a state of complete absorption when the challenge level matches their skill level. Too easy → boredom. Too hard → anxiety. The sweet spot → flow.

**Your real-time job is to keep the student in the flow channel.**

This means adjusting difficulty *during* the conversation:

| Signal | Meaning | Your Move |
|--------|---------|-----------|
| Student answering fluently, short responses, disengaged tone | Too easy | Raise complexity: ask "why" questions, introduce hypotheticals, shift to abstract topics |
| Student freezing, long silences, visible stress | Too hard | Lower complexity: return to familiar territory, ask about their day, use closed questions temporarily |
| Student speaking freely, elaborating unprompted, losing track of time | Flow state | Change nothing. You are in the zone. Ride it. |

**The 70-80% Rule:** Aim for the student to succeed approximately 70-80% of the time. If they nail every question, you are too easy and the assessment ceiling is invisible. If they struggle with more than 30%, the filter goes up and the data degrades.

**Flow markers that indicate a successful session:**
- "Wait, is it over already?" (temporal distortion — hallmark of flow)
- Student continues talking after you signal the session is ending
- Student volunteers information you did not ask for
- Student forgets to be nervous

This is Csikszentmihalyi meeting Krashen: flow state = low affective filter = accurate acquisition data. The three frameworks converge on the same session design.

### Kahneman: System 1, System 2, and Why You Must Not Trigger Thinking

Daniel Kahneman's dual-process theory: **System 1** is fast, automatic, intuitive. **System 2** is slow, deliberate, analytical.

Natural language production is System 1. Grammar self-correction is System 2. You want System 1.

**What triggers System 2 (avoid these):**
- Asking the student to explain *why* they chose a particular word or structure
- Drawing attention to a grammatical choice: "Interesting — you used the subjunctive there"
- Any metalinguistic commentary during the session ("Your vocabulary is quite advanced")
- Formal test-like framing: "Question three..." or "Now we'll move to the reading section"
- Price discussions during the assessment (money activates System 2 immediately)

**What keeps the student in System 1 (do these):**
- Conversation about real life: their work, their weekend, their opinions
- Follow-up questions that feel natural, not diagnostic
- Emotional topics (appropriately): travel stories, childhood memories, future plans
- Humour, anecdotes, genuine reactions from you
- The Late-Night FM DJ Voice (Voss) — this is a System 1 signal. It says "we are chatting," not "you are being tested"

**Kahneman on product mentions:**
Loss framing ("You'll miss out if you don't...") is a System 2 trigger that activates analytical resistance. Gain framing ("Available if you want to go deeper") keeps the student in a receptive, low-effort state. This is why Camp CTAs are Level 5 — they are designed to be processed by System 1 without triggering System 2 defenses.

### How the Four Frameworks Converge

| Framework | Core Instruction | What It Protects |
|-----------|-----------------|------------------|
| Berne | Operate from Adult, invite Free Child | Authentic self-expression |
| Krashen | Keep the affective filter down | Acquisition data (not performance data) |
| Csikszentmihalyi | Match challenge to skill in real-time | Engagement and accurate ceiling detection |
| Kahneman | Keep the student in System 1 | Natural language production |

All four say the same thing in different languages: **the moment the student starts thinking about what they are doing, you have lost the signal.** Camp is the behavioural system that prevents this. Now you know why.

## 3. BYAF Compliance

BYAF — **"But You Are Free"** — is the single most validated compliance technique in behavioural science. A meta-analysis of 42 studies (Carpenter, 2013) found that simply reminding someone they are free to choose increases compliance from approximately 10% to 47.5%.

### The Rule

**Every recommendation, suggestion, or mention of a product or service must be followed by an explicit acknowledgement of the student's freedom to decline.**

This is not optional. This is not "when it feels natural." This is every time.

### What BYAF Sounds Like

| Without BYAF (violation) | With BYAF (compliant) |
|--------------------------|----------------------|
| "You should book a reassessment in eight weeks." | "Some students find it useful to reassess after eight weeks. Entirely up to you." |
| "The homework check would really help you." | "We have a homework check if you want to work on this between now and your next session. If not, the report itself has plenty to work with." |
| "I'd recommend the Full Assessment." | "If you wanted a deeper look, the Full Assessment goes into more detail. But the Quick gives you a solid picture too." |
| "You need more practice." | "More practice tends to close this kind of gap. How you choose to get that practice is completely your call." |

### The Anatomy of a BYAF Statement

1. **Present the option** — factual, specific, no superlatives
2. **Acknowledge the alternative** — there is always a valid alternative to buying
3. **Hand back the decision** — "entirely up to you," "your call," "if it feels right"

### Common BYAF Mistakes

- **Passive-aggressive BYAF:** "You're free to ignore this, but..." — This is worse than no BYAF at all. The "but" negates everything before it.
- **Performative BYAF:** Saying "it's up to you" in a tone that clearly communicates "you'd be an idiot not to." Tone must match words.
- **Missing BYAF:** Forgetting to add the freedom acknowledgement because the conversation flowed naturally. Natural flow does not override the rule. Add it.

## 4. Self-Selection Upsells

### The Principle

The best upsell is the one the student asks for. The second best is the one they select from options you present without advocacy. The worst is the one you push.

LingoGrade upsells are designed as **self-selection paths** — the student sees what exists, understands what each option does, and chooses based on their own assessment of value.

### The Three-Option Architecture

When presenting products or next steps, always use three options with ascending commitment:

| Low | Medium | High |
|-----|--------|------|
| Free / Already included | Core product | Premium |
| "Your report includes..." | "If you want to go deeper..." | "For the complete picture..." |
| No action required | One decision | Bigger investment |

The middle option is where most people land. This is Thaler's choice architecture — not manipulation, but thoughtful design that helps people make decisions they feel good about.

### How Self-Selection Works in Practice

**After delivering assessment results:**

"Your report will have all of this broken down — that's included. (Pause.)

Some students also find the homework check useful. It's a written exercise based on your specific patterns, and you get detailed corrections back within 55 minutes. That's 29.95.

And if you want to track how this changes over time, the reassessment at eight weeks gives you a before-and-after comparison.

All optional. The report on its own gives you a clear picture of where you stand."

**What just happened:**
1. Free option stated first (lowers guard)
2. Mid-tier product described with specific benefit (not generic "it helps")
3. Premium option connected to a future vision (tracking change over time)
4. Explicit BYAF close ("all optional")
5. Safety valve ("report on its own gives you a clear picture")

### What You Never Do

- Never recommend one option over another: "I'd go with the Full if I were you."
- Never imply the free option is insufficient: "The Quick Assessment is just a starting point."
- Never create a false binary: "You can either book more sessions or stay at this level."
- Never mention price before value: "For 29.95 you can get..." — always describe what it is first.
- Never stack more than three options. Decision fatigue kills conversion.

### The Silence After

After presenting options, **stop talking.** Count to five in your head. The silence is where the student processes. If you fill it with more information, you are selling. If you hold it, you are presenting.

Voss's Law of Silence: the most persuasive thing you can do after stating value is nothing.

## 5. CTA Optimization

A CTA (Call to Action) is any moment where you invite the student to take a next step. In LingoGrade, every CTA must meet **Camp Level 5** — the student must feel they are choosing, never being directed.

### The Camp Level Scale

| Level | Description | Example |
|-------|-------------|---------|
| 1 | Command | "Book your reassessment now." |
| 2 | Strong suggestion | "You should definitely do the homework check." |
| 3 | Recommendation with rationale | "I'd recommend the reassessment — most students see real progress." |
| 4 | Presented option | "The reassessment is available if you want to track this over time." |
| **5** | **Self-selection with freedom** | **"Would it be wrong to see how these patterns change in eight weeks? If not interesting, no problem."** |

**Levels 1-3 are violations.** Level 4 is acceptable in casual mention. Level 5 is the standard for any deliberate product reference.

### No-Oriented Questions

Camp's signature tool. A no-oriented question frames the desired action as something the student would say "no" to rejecting:

- "Would it be unreasonable to check on this in eight weeks?"
- "Would it be wrong to have someone look at your written work?"
- "Would it be crazy to see how far you've come?"

The student's natural response is "No, that's not unreasonable" — which is functionally a yes, but one that feels like their own conclusion.

### CTA Language Standards

**Banned words and phrases:**
- "Buy," "purchase," "order," "grab," "claim," "act now," "don't miss"
- "Only" before a price ("only 29.95" — minimizing implies you think they'll resist)
- "Should," "need to," "have to," "must"
- "Limited time," "while supplies last," "before it's too late"
- "I recommend," "I suggest," "trust me"

**Preferred language:**
- "Available," "exists," "option," "if you want," "whenever you're ready"
- "Some students find it useful," "a common next step is"
- "Entirely up to you," "your call," "if it feels right"
- Describe, pause, let them decide

### The Post-Session CTA Sequence

At the end of a session, you have a natural window for mentioning next steps. Here is the Camp-compliant sequence:

1. **Close the session with warmth** (see Assessor Rules, Section 2, The Close)
2. **State what they will receive for free:** "Your full report will be in your inbox within [timeframe]."
3. **One optional mention, maximum:** "If you want to dig deeper into the writing side, the homework check exists. If not, the report covers everything."
4. **BYAF close:** "Take your time with the report. Everything is there when you're ready."
5. **End.** No second mention. No "just to remind you." No follow-up nudge.

If the student asks about other products — answer honestly, factually, and briefly. Then stop.

## 6. Fake Scarcity & What We Never Do

### The Data on Fake Scarcity

Short-term: fake urgency can boost conversion by up to 217% (7th Growth, 2024).
Long-term: it destroys trust, tanks lifetime value, and generates negative word-of-mouth that compounds for years. Over 60% of online shoppers test urgency claims by refreshing the page. When the "limited offer" is still there, trust drops permanently.

LingoGrade is a premium brand serving discerning adults. Our students are professionals, parents, academics, and executives. They recognise manipulation. They punish it by leaving silently and telling their network.

### The Scarcity Policy

**Real scarcity — state it factually:**
- "Assessor availability in Zurich is limited to three slots this week." (If true.)
- "The reassessment window is eight weeks from your original session." (Structural, not artificial.)

**Fake scarcity — never, under any circumstance:**
- "This discount expires soon!" (If it doesn't have a real, published end date, it's fake.)
- "Only a few spots left!" (Unless you have verified calendar data showing this.)
- "Prices are going up!" (Unless a published price change is scheduled.)
- Countdown timers of any kind.
- "Last chance" language.
- Implying that something always available is temporarily available.

### The Pressure Policy

**You do not create urgency. You observe reality.**

If a student genuinely has a time-sensitive situation (a job interview in two weeks, an exam in a month), you can acknowledge that reality:
- "Given your timeline, the earlier you start working on this, the more time you have. But there's no rush from our side."

Notice: the urgency comes from their situation, not from you. You are a mirror, not a countdown clock.

### What We Also Never Do

- **Guilt framing:** "Don't you want to improve?" / "Your children deserve better English." / "You owe it to yourself."
- **Social pressure:** "Everyone else books the full package." / "Most people your level..."
- **Fear of missing out:** "Other students at your company have already booked."
- **Sunk cost leverage:** "You've already invested in the assessment, so you might as well..."
- **Conditional warmth:** Being noticeably warmer to students who buy more. Every student receives identical warmth regardless of spend.

## 7. Session Scripts — Before, During, After

These are not scripts to memorise. They are templates showing Camp-compliant language for common moments. Adapt to your natural voice.

### Before the Session

**Confirmation message (automated, but you should know the tone):**
> "Your session is confirmed for [date] at [time]. No preparation needed — just show up as you are. If anything comes up, you can reschedule up to 48 hours before. See you then."

No "we can't wait!" No exclamation marks stacked. Calm, factual, warm.

### Session Opening

**Camp-compliant opener:**
> "Hey [name]. Before we get into anything — how's your day going?"

Then listen. Actually listen. Whatever they say, mirror one detail back. This is not a warm-up exercise. This is you demonstrating that you see them as a person, not a revenue line.

**If the student is nervous:**
> "There is genuinely nothing you can get wrong here. If you don't know a word, say it in your language. If you blank completely, we just move on. This is a conversation, not a test."

This is Lozanov's de-suggestion. You are removing the barrier before it can form.

### During the Session — Handling Product Questions

**Student asks: "Should I do the homework check?"**
> "It depends on what you want. If you're someone who likes working on things between sessions, it gives you structured practice with corrections. If you prefer to absorb the report and work at your own pace, that works too. Both are valid."

**Student asks: "What's the difference between Quick and Full?"**
> "The Quick is 15 minutes — gives you a clear snapshot. The Full is 25 minutes — goes deeper into patterns and includes written analysis. The report is more detailed. The Quick is enough for most people who want a clear picture. The Full is there if you want the full landscape."

Notice: no advocacy. No "I'd go with." Present, describe, hand back.

**Student asks: "Is it worth it?"**
> "That depends entirely on what 'worth it' means for you. I can tell you what it includes. Whether that's valuable to you — only you can answer that."

This is the hardest moment for most assessors. The instinct screams "YES, it's worth it, let me convince you!" Resist. The student is testing whether you will sell them or respect them. Pass the test.

### After the Session

**Closing — no product mention:**
> "This was genuinely enjoyable. You'll get your report within [timeframe]. Take your time with it — there's a lot in there. If questions come up after you read it, you can reach out anytime."

**Closing — one natural product mention:**
> "Your report will cover everything we discussed. If you want to work on the writing patterns we noticed, the homework check exists — but the report itself gives you plenty to work with either way. It was great talking with you."

**Never:**
> "So, are you thinking about the homework check? Or maybe the reassessment? We also have lesson packages..."

This is a menu recital. It is Camp Level 1. It communicates desperation.

## 8. The Report as Camp Document

The report the student receives is the most powerful sales tool LingoGrade has — precisely because it is not a sales tool. It is a diagnostic document. Its quality, specificity, and honesty generate more rebookings than any CTA ever could.

### Report Tone Rules

- **Present, don't push.** "Your performance aligns with B1" — not "You are B1" (identity label) or "You only reached B1" (judgment).
- **Corrections are patterns, not mistakes.** "A recurring pattern in your speech: [specific example]" — not "You made this error repeatedly."
- **Solutions are options, not prescriptions.** "Speakers who resolved similar patterns typically used..." — not "You need to practice..."
- **Lozanov language:** Never use "error" or "mistake" in student-facing copy. Use "pattern," "focus area," "growth point," or "developing structure."

### Upsell Boxes in Reports

Reports include contextual offer boxes based on what the student hasn't purchased. These boxes follow strict rules:

1. **Green background** — warm, non-urgent
2. **Native language** — always in the student's language, not the assessed language
3. **Observational framing:** "Available if you'd like to go deeper" — not "Don't miss this opportunity"
4. **One box per product, maximum three boxes per report**
5. **Safety valve on every box:** "Not interested? No action needed."
6. **Price stated once, factually:** "Homework Check — 29.95" — no "only," no "just," no "starting at"

### The Killer CTA

The single most effective CTA in all of LingoGrade is a no-oriented question placed at the end of the reassessment section of the report:

> "Would it be wrong to see how these patterns change in eight weeks?"

This works because:
- It is a genuine question, not a command
- "No, it wouldn't be wrong" is naturally the reader's response
- It connects to a future vision (seeing change)
- It does not mention price, booking, or any transactional element
- It respects Camp Pillar 3 (Let Them Say No)

## 9. Common Violations & How to Fix Them

### Violation 1: The Enthusiasm Trap

**What it looks like:** "Oh, you'd LOVE the homework check! It's perfect for exactly what you're working on!"

**Why it's a violation:** Your enthusiasm signals investment in the outcome. The student now feels obligated to match your energy or disappoint you. This is Camp Level 2.

**The fix:** "The homework check targets exactly the patterns we discussed. It's there if you want it." (Neutral tone. Factual. No exclamation marks in your voice.)

### Violation 2: The Soft Push

**What it looks like:** "I'm not trying to sell you anything, but..." or "I know you probably don't want to hear this, but..."

**Why it's a violation:** Pre-framing a statement as "not a sell" is a sell. The meta-communication is: "I know what I'm about to say is pushy, so I'll pre-empt your resistance." The student's guard goes up immediately.

**The fix:** Don't announce that you're not selling. Just don't sell. State facts. Stop talking. Let the student lead.

### Violation 3: The Assumptive Close

**What it looks like:** "So, shall I put you down for the reassessment in eight weeks?" or "I'll send you the homework check link."

**Why it's a violation:** You have assumed the student's decision before they made it. This is Camp Level 1 — a command disguised as a question.

**The fix:** "The reassessment is available at eight weeks if you decide you want to track this. Your report will have all the details."

### Violation 4: The Comparison Trap

**What it looks like:** "Most students at your level book the Full Assessment" or "The Quick is okay, but the Full gives you so much more."

**Why it's a violation:** "Most students" is social pressure (Cialdini, but used as a weapon rather than a tool). Comparing tiers with implicit hierarchy ("okay, but") devalues the cheaper option and pressures the upgrade.

**The fix:** Describe each option on its own merits. Never use one to diminish another. "The Quick gives you X. The Full gives you X plus Y." No "but." No "most people."

### Violation 5: The Follow-Up Nudge

**What it looks like:** After the session, sending a personal message: "Hey, just checking in — did you see the homework check option in your report?"

**Why it's a violation:** This is pursuit. It communicates that you need them to buy. The automated drip system handles follow-up with Camp-compliant copy. You do not supplement it with personal outreach about products. If you want to follow up personally, make it about the student, not about a purchase: "Hope you found the report useful. Let me know if any questions come up."

**The fix:** Trust the system. Trust the report. Trust silence.

### Violation 6: Conditional Warmth

**What it looks like:** Being noticeably more engaged with students who mention they want to book more, or visibly deflating when a student says "I just want the Quick."

**Why it's a violation:** Students detect this instantly. The message received is: "My value to this person is my wallet." This is the opposite of Camp.

**The fix:** The student who books a Quick Assessment and never returns should receive the exact same quality, warmth, and attention as the student who books a DeepDive, all homework checks, and a reassessment. Identical. No exceptions.

## 10. Self-Assessment Checklist

After every session, review these questions honestly. If you answer "no" to any of them, identify the moment where it went wrong and plan to correct it next time.

### Camp Compliance

- [ ] Did I enter the session with zero investment in whether the student buys anything?
- [ ] Did I present options without advocating for any specific one?
- [ ] Did every product mention include a BYAF acknowledgement?
- [ ] Did I use no-oriented questions rather than direct suggestions?
- [ ] Did I respect all four budgets (time, energy, money, emotion)?
- [ ] Did I hold silence after presenting options (minimum 5 seconds)?

### Scarcity & Pressure

- [ ] Did I avoid creating any artificial urgency?
- [ ] Did I avoid guilt framing?
- [ ] Did I avoid social pressure ("most students...")?
- [ ] Did I state prices factually, without minimising language ("only," "just")?

### Tone & Posture

- [ ] Was my talk-to-listen ratio approximately 30/70?
- [ ] Did I avoid the Enthusiasm Trap?
- [ ] Did I avoid the Soft Push ("I'm not trying to sell you, but...")?
- [ ] Did I close with warmth regardless of whether the student showed purchase intent?
- [ ] Would I be comfortable if this session were reviewed by the student's family?

### The Final Test

"If I removed every product mention from this session, would it still have been a valuable experience for the student?"

If yes — you are in Camp.

If no — the session depended on the sell, and that means the sell compromised the session.

## Appendix: Camp Protocol Quick Reference Card

Print this. Keep it visible during sessions until it becomes instinct.

| Situation | Camp Response |
|-----------|--------------|
| Student asks "What should I do next?" | "That depends on what matters most to you. Here are the options..." |

| Student declines everything | "The report has everything you need. It was great talking with you." |
