## 1. Mindset: The Tactical Empath

> *"Negotiation is not an act of battle; it's a process of discovery."*
> -- Chris Voss

You are not a test administrator. You are not a gatekeeper. You are a **mirror** -- a calm, warm surface in which the student discovers their own linguistic reality. Every session you run is a negotiation: the student arrives defending an identity ("I'm bad at languages" or "I'm actually pretty good"), and your job is to guide them past that defence into honest self-awareness, without ever making them feel attacked.

### Core Principles

**Tactical Empathy**
Tactical empathy is not sympathy. It is not agreeing with the student. It is the disciplined act of understanding their emotional state and demonstrating that understanding openly. When a student stumbles on a conjugation, they feel it before you say anything. Your job is to acknowledge that feeling before it hardens into shame.

- "It sounds like that one caught you off guard."
- "It seems like you knew the word but it slipped away."

These are **labels** -- Voss's tool for defusing negative emotions by naming them. A labelled emotion loses its power. An unlabelled one festers.

**Mirroring**
Repeat the last one to three words the student says, with a slight upward inflection. This is the simplest and most powerful tool in your kit. It keeps the student talking. It makes them feel heard. It buys you processing time.

Student: "I always freeze when I have to speak in real situations."
You: "Real situations?"
Student: (continues, opens up, gives you diagnostic gold)

Mirroring is not parroting. It is surgical. You select the words that carry emotional weight and reflect them back.

**The Late-Night FM DJ Voice**
Voss calls it exactly that. Lower your pitch. Slow your cadence. Not monotone -- warm, deliberate, calm. This voice signals safety. It tells the student's nervous system: "There is no threat here." A student in fight-or-flight cannot demonstrate their true language ability. Your voice is the instrument that moves them out of anxiety and into performance.

**The Accumulation of Small Agreements**
Never push for a big "yes." Instead, build a chain of micro-acknowledgements:

- "Does that make sense?"
- "Is that fair to say?"
- "Would it be reasonable to look at it that way?"

Each small "yes" lowers resistance. By the time you deliver the assessment result, the student has already been agreeing with the trajectory for ten minutes.

### The Invisible War

Sun Tzu wrote: *"The supreme art of war is to subdue the enemy without fighting."* Your "enemy" is not the student. It is their **ego defence**. The student who insists they are B2 when they are A2 is not lying to you -- they are protecting themselves. You do not assault the fortress. You walk around it.

## 2. The 15-Minute Session

> *"All men can see the tactics whereby I conquer, but what none can see is the strategy out of which victory is evolved."*
> -- Sun Tzu

Fifteen minutes is not a limitation. It is a **design constraint** that produces intensity, focus, and memorability. A 60-minute assessment allows the student to coast. Fifteen minutes demands that every second earns its place.

### Structure

| Time | Phase | Purpose |
|------|-------|---------|
| 0:00 -- 1:30 | **The Opening (Warm-Up)** | Build rapport. Activate the Free Child. |
| 1:30 -- 5:00 | **Guided Conversation** | Assess fluency, vocabulary range, natural speech patterns. |
| 5:00 -- 9:00 | **Diagnostic Probing** | Targeted questions to identify ceiling and floor of ability. |
| 9:00 -- 12:00 | **Challenge Zone** | Push slightly beyond comfort. Observe error patterns under pressure. |
| 12:00 -- 15:00 | **The Close** | Summarise observations. Deliver initial verbal impression. Plant seeds. |

### Phase Details

**The Opening (0:00 -- 1:30)**
Your first words set the tone for everything. Do not begin with "So, let's test your German." Begin with something human:

- "Before we start -- how's your day been?"
- "I noticed you booked from [city]. What's the weather like there today?"

This is not small talk. This is **Lozanov's de-suggestion** in action. The student arrives carrying the emotional baggage of every bad language class they have ever sat through. Your opening must signal: *This is not that.* You are not their old teacher. There is no red pen here.

The goal is to activate what Eric Berne called the **Free Child** ego state -- the spontaneous, creative, unguarded self. The Free Child is where authentic language production lives. The Adapted Child (obedient, anxious, performing for approval) produces rehearsed phrases. The Free Child produces real speech.

How to trigger the Free Child:
- Laugh genuinely (not performatively).
- Share something small and real about yourself.
- Ask about something they care about, not something "educational."
- Use their name. Not constantly. Once or twice, placed well.

**Guided Conversation (1:30 -- 5:00)**
Follow the student's energy. If they light up talking about cooking, stay with cooking. Krashen's Acquisition Theory tells us that language is acquired when the learner is focused on meaning, not form. A student describing their grandmother's recipe is acquiring. A student reciting verb tables is studying. These are not the same thing.

During this phase, you are silently cataloguing:
- Sentence complexity (simple, compound, complex)
- Verb tense range (do they stay in present? Can they narrate past events?)
- Vocabulary breadth and precision
- Self-correction patterns (do they notice their own errors?)
- Filler words and hesitation markers

Do not correct errors during this phase. Note them. Let the student flow.

**Diagnostic Probing (5:00 -- 9:00)**
Now you test the edges. Use **calibrated questions** -- Voss's open-ended questions that begin with "How" or "What":

- "What would you do if you had to explain this to a colleague in [target language]?"
- "How would you describe your ideal weekend -- but in past tense, as if it already happened?"

These questions force production at specific grammatical levels without making the student feel tested. They think they are answering a question. They are actually performing a diagnostic task.

**Challenge Zone (9:00 -- 12:00)**
Gently increase difficulty. Introduce a hypothetical scenario. Ask them to persuade you of something. Request a brief summary of a topic they just discussed, but from a different angle.

Watch for the **ceiling** -- the point where errors become systematic rather than incidental. That ceiling is the true level.

If the student struggles visibly, **label it immediately**:
- "It seems like that structure is just on the edge of what feels comfortable."
- "It sounds like you know what you want to say but the grammar is getting in the way."

This prevents shame. The student feels seen, not exposed.

**The Close (12:00 -- 15:00)**
Never end with a score. End with a **feeling**.

- Summarise two or three things they did well. Be specific. Not "Your German is good" but "The way you switched between past and present tense when talking about your trip -- that was smooth."
- Mention one area for growth, framed as opportunity: "The subjunctive is sitting right there waiting for you. Once that clicks, your German jumps a full level."
- If appropriate, mention the detailed report they will receive. Do not sell it. State it: "You'll get a full written report with all of this broken down."

The last thing they hear should make them feel **capable and curious**, not defeated.

## 3. Equipment Standards

> *"Amateurs talk strategy. Professionals talk logistics."*
> -- General Omar Bradley

Your audio and video quality communicate professionalism before you open your mouth. A student who sees a grainy webcam and hears tinny audio has already made a judgement about LingoGrade's quality. That judgement is unfair, and it is also permanent.

### Audio -- Non-Negotiable

| Requirement | Standard |
|------------|----------|
| **Microphone** | **Shure MV7+ minimum.** This is not a suggestion. This is the floor. No exceptions, no alternatives, no "but my Blue Yeti sounds fine." The MV7+ delivers broadcast-grade audio that communicates authority and care. The student hears warmth, clarity, and presence -- not room echo and keyboard clicks. |
| **Microphone Position** | 6-8 inches from mouth. Slightly off-axis (not directly in front of lips) to reduce plosives. |
| **Room Treatment** | At minimum: a treated corner with acoustic panels or heavy blankets behind and to the sides. No hard, reflective surfaces behind you. If your room echoes when you clap, it is not ready. |
| **Monitoring** | Use closed-back headphones during sessions. You must hear what the student hears, not what your room adds. |

### Video

| Requirement | Standard |
|------------|----------|
| **Camera** | 1080p minimum. 4K preferred. Built-in laptop cameras are acceptable only if they meet the resolution standard and produce clean, well-lit images. External webcams (Logitech C920/C922 or better) are recommended. |
| **Frame** | Head and upper shoulders visible. Camera at eye level or slightly above. Never below -- nobody looks authoritative from below. |
| **Background** | Clean, uncluttered, professional. A bookshelf is fine. A pile of laundry is not. Solid colour or subtle texture preferred. Virtual backgrounds are prohibited -- they glitch, they look cheap, they break immersion. |
| **Lighting** | Key light in front of you, slightly above and to one side. No overhead-only lighting (creates under-eye shadows). Ring lights are acceptable. Natural window light is acceptable if consistent. Backlighting (window behind you) is never acceptable. |

### Internet

| Requirement | Standard |
|------------|----------|
| **Speed** | 50 Mbps download / 20 Mbps upload minimum. |
| **Connection** | Wired Ethernet strongly preferred. If Wi-Fi, ensure 5GHz band and strong signal. |
| **Backup** | Have a mobile hotspot ready. If your connection drops mid-session, you have 60 seconds to reconnect before the session is flagged. |

### Your Environment

- **Silence.** No barking dogs, no street noise, no housemates. If you cannot guarantee 15 minutes of silence, you cannot run the session.
- **Do Not Disturb.** Phone on silent. Notifications off. All non-essential applications closed. The student is paying for your undivided attention.

## 4. Dress Code

> *"A prince must be a fox to recognise traps, and a lion to frighten wolves."*
> -- Niccolo Machiavelli

You are not a corporate drone and you are not a beach instructor. You sit in the space between: **professional enough to be taken seriously, human enough to be trusted.**

### Standards

- **Solid colours preferred.** Avoid busy patterns, logos, and stripes (they create moire on camera).
- **LingoGrade brand colours encouraged** where tasteful. Subtle incorporation -- a scarf, a pen, a mug visible on desk -- reinforces brand without looking like a uniform.
- **Collared shirts, smart knitwear, or clean crew-neck tops.** No hoodies. No visible gym wear.
- **Grooming matters.** Hair tidy. If you have a beard, keep it shaped. This is a video call, not a podcast recording in your garage.
- **Jewellery and accessories:** Minimal. Nothing that clinks against the desk or catches light distractingly.

The principle: **if a student's parent saw a screenshot of your session, would they feel their money was well spent?** If the answer is not an immediate yes, change something.

## 5. Communication Rules

> *"He who has learned to disagree without being disagreeable has discovered the most valuable secret of negotiation."*
> -- Robert Estabrook

### Delivering Difficult News (Low Scores)

This is where most assessors fail. The instinct is to soften, hedge, or inflate. We do none of these. We use **Voss's accusation audit** -- front-loading the negatives so the student braces for worse than what comes.

**The Formula:**

Step 1 -- **Accusation Audit.** Before delivering the assessment, acknowledge what the student might be feeling:
- "You might feel like this is going to be harsh."
- "You're probably expecting me to tell you something you don't want to hear."

This defuses the emotional bomb before it detonates. The student braces for a C1 and hears B1, but because you pre-loaded the discomfort, the B1 lands softer than it would have otherwise.

Step 2 -- **Label the gap, not the failure.**
Do not say: "Your grammar is weak."
Say: "There seems to be a gap between your conversational fluency -- which is genuinely strong -- and your grammatical accuracy. That gap is extremely common at your stage, and it's one of the most fixable things in language learning."

Step 3 -- **The "That's Right" Moment.**
Voss teaches that the two most powerful words in negotiation are "that's right" -- not "you're right" (which is dismissive), but "that's right" (which means genuine agreement). Your goal is to deliver the assessment in such a way that the student says, or clearly feels, "That's right. That's exactly where I am."

When a student recognises their own level in your description, resistance dissolves. They do not feel judged. They feel **understood**.

Step 4 -- **The Bridge.**
Never leave the student at the bad news. Always connect it to a path forward:
- "The good news is that this is exactly the kind of gap that targeted practice closes fast."
- "Students at your level who commit to even 15 minutes a day tend to see measurable progress within weeks."

This is not upselling. This is hope grounded in honesty. The upsell happens naturally -- a student who feels understood and sees a clear path will seek out the tools to walk it.

### How to Upsell Without Selling

**You do not sell. You describe what exists.**

- "We do have a lesson programme built around exactly this kind of targeted gap-closing." (Pause. Let them ask.)
- "Some students find the homework check useful for this -- it's a 55-minute turnaround on written exercises." (State fact. Stop talking.)

Voss's **Law of Silence**: after you make a statement of value, stop. The silence creates space for the student to step forward. If you fill that silence, you steal their agency and they feel pushed.

The best upsell is the one the student asks for.

### Language and Tone

- **Never use jargon with the student** unless you immediately explain it. "Subjunctive" means nothing to a beginner. "That way of expressing wishes and hypotheticals" does.
- **Never say "but."** Replace with "and." "Your vocabulary is strong *but* your grammar needs work" lands as a dismissal. "Your vocabulary is strong *and* your grammar is the next frontier" lands as a roadmap.
- **Use the student's name.** Sparingly. Placed at moments of encouragement or gentle correction. It signals attention.
- **Match their energy, then lead it.** If they are nervous, start at their speed, then gradually slow down. They will follow. This is called pacing and leading in NLP, and it works.

## 6. Ethics

> *"The reputation of a thousand years may be determined by the conduct of one hour."*
> -- Japanese Proverb

### The Iron Rules

1. **Never inflate a score.** Not to make the student happy. Not to avoid confrontation. Not to improve your tip rate. A student who receives an inflated B2 and then fails a B2 job interview will never trust LingoGrade again. They will also tell fifteen people. Honesty is not just ethical -- it is strategic. An honest assessment that stings today builds a reputation that compounds for years.

2. **Never deflate a score.** Not to create dependency. Not to make them feel they need more sessions. This is the inverse corruption and it is equally unacceptable.

3. **Student privacy is absolute.** Session recordings, notes, assessment data, and personal information are never shared with anyone outside the LingoGrade platform. Not with other assessors. Not on social media. Not as anonymised "funny stories." If it happened in a session, it stays in the session.

4. **No personal relationships with active students.** You are a professional assessor. Friendly is required. Friends is a conflict of interest. If a personal relationship develops, disclose it immediately and the student will be reassigned.

5. **No external solicitation.** You do not offer private lessons outside LingoGrade. You do not redirect students to your personal tutoring business. You are being paid for your expertise within this platform. Violation is grounds for immediate termination.

6. **Report accuracy.** The DOCX/PDF report that the student receives must reflect what actually happened in the session. Do not fabricate examples. Do not copy-paste generic feedback. Every report is a **personalised document** -- the student's name, their specific errors, their specific strengths, their specific next steps.

7. **Bias awareness.** Accent, nationality, age, gender, appearance -- none of these affect a language level. Assess the language. Nothing else.

### When in Doubt

If you encounter a situation not covered by these rules, apply this test: *"If this interaction were recorded and shown to the student's family, my manager, and a journalist, would I be comfortable with every word I said and every decision I made?"*

If the answer is yes, proceed. If the answer is anything else, stop and ask.

## 7. Earning & Tips

> *"Do not go where the path may lead; go instead where there is no path and leave a trail."*
> -- Ralph Waldo Emerson

### Commission Structure

Your base compensation per session is outlined in your assessor agreement. It reflects the premium positioning of LingoGrade and the expertise we require.

### Tips: 100% Yours

**LingoGrade takes a 0% cut on tips. Zero. Every cent a student tips goes directly to you.** This is not a promotional offer. This is a permanent, non-negotiable policy. We believe that if a student values your work enough to express it financially, that expression belongs entirely to you.

### How to Earn Tips (Without Asking)

You will never ask for a tip. You will never hint at a tip. You will never make a student feel that tipping is expected. What you will do is deliver sessions of such quality that the student feels compelled to express gratitude.

The formula is simple:

1. **Be the best 15 minutes of their day.** Not the most stressful. Not the most educational. The best. The session they tell someone about at dinner.

2. **Remember details.** If a student mentions their daughter's name in session one, ask about her in session two. This is not manipulation -- this is the baseline of human decency that most professionals fail to clear.

3. **Exceed the deliverable.** The report should contain at least one insight the student did not expect. One observation that makes them think: "They really were paying attention."

4. **Close with warmth, not formality.** "It was genuinely good to meet you" lands differently than "Thank you for your time." Mean it or don't say it.

5. **Let Marco and Mila do their work.** The owl mascots (Marco for male assessors, Mila for female assessors) appear throughout the student's experience -- in reports, in follow-ups, in the platform itself. When you reference Marco or Mila naturally ("Marco's going to have some interesting notes for your report"), you reinforce the brand personality and the student's emotional connection to the experience.

Tips follow excellence the way shadows follow objects. You cannot chase them. You can only stand in the light.

## 8. The Greene Principles

> *"The greatest victory is that which requires no battle."*
> -- Sun Tzu

Robert Greene's *48 Laws of Power* were written about courts and kings, but the dynamics they describe are universal. Three laws are especially relevant to assessment work.

### Law 1: Never Outshine the Master

In our context: **Never make the student feel stupid.**

The "master" in an assessment is not you -- it is the student's self-image. They have walked into this session with a story about who they are linguistically. Your job is not to demolish that story. It is to **refine** it.

- When a student makes an error, do not pounce. Note it silently.
- When a student overestimates their level, do not contradict immediately. Guide them into a task that reveals the gap naturally. Let them discover it. A truth discovered is accepted. A truth imposed is resisted.
- When a student underestimates their level, celebrate it. "You said you were a beginner, but you just used three different past tenses in one sentence. That's not beginner behaviour."

The student should leave feeling **more intelligent than when they arrived** -- not because you flattered them, but because you showed them what they already had.

### Law 4: Always Say Less Than Necessary

In our context: **Let the student talk.**

You are assessing their language production. You cannot assess what you cannot hear. Every second you spend talking is a second of diagnostic data you are not collecting.

Practical application:
- Ask a question. Then **wait**. Count to five in your head if you must. Silence is not awkward -- it is space for the student to think in the target language.
- Do not finish their sentences. The pause before they find the word is diagnostically rich. If you supply the word, you have stolen data from yourself and agency from them.
- When you do speak, speak with precision. One well-placed observation is worth ten minutes of chatter. The scalpel, not the sledgehammer.
- Your talk-to-listen ratio should be approximately **30/70**. If you are talking more than the student, something has gone wrong.

### Law 9: Win Through Your Actions, Never Through Argument

In our context: **Let the report speak.**

If a student disagrees with their assessment verbally during the session, do not argue. Do not defend. Do not pull out evidence like a prosecutor. Simply say:

- "That's a fair perspective. The report will lay everything out with specific examples -- I think it'll make the picture clearer."

Then deliver a report so thorough, so detailed, so evidently rooted in what actually happened in the session, that the assessment becomes self-evident. The student reads their own errors quoted back to them. They see the pattern you identified. They recognise it.

No argument can achieve what a well-written report achieves in silence.

### The Deeper Principle

Beneath all of Greene's laws is a single truth: **power flows to those who create value without demanding recognition.** The assessor who needs the student to agree in real-time is insecure. The assessor who delivers quietly excellent work and lets the output speak is formidable.

Be formidable.

## Final Words

You are not a test proctor. You are not a language teacher. You are not a salesperson.

You are a **diagnostic artist** -- someone who, in fifteen minutes, can map the landscape of another person's linguistic ability with precision, empathy, and grace. The student may not remember every word you said. They will remember how you made them feel.

Make them feel understood. Make them feel capable. Make them feel that someone finally saw their language ability clearly -- not to judge it, but to help them grow it.

That is the work. Everything else is logistics.
